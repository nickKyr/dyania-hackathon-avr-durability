# Presentation — content and spoken script

> **How to use this file.** This is the content of the deck, written as it is meant to be said:
> eight slides, five minutes, then two minutes of panel questions. Build `slides.pdf` from it and
> put the PDF in this folder. Timings are cumulative and add to 5:00, which means the script is
> deliberately short — a five-minute pitch read aloud is about 700 words, and this is 700 words.
>
> **Names and roles are left blank on slide 1 for the team to fill in.** Everything else is
> written from what is in this repository, and every figure quoted here is regenerated by a script
> in [`../scripts/`](../scripts/) and carries the commit it came from.

**The one sentence, if only one survives:** *a bioprosthetic valve is watched by the calendar, but
it does not fail by the calendar — and the patient nobody has imaged is not the patient at low
risk.*

---

## Slide 1 — Title (0:15)

**On the slide:** Predicting bioprosthetic aortic valve durability · Team `dyanooumenoi` ·
*(names and roles)* · Dyania Health Hackathon, Athens, September 2026.

**Said:** "We were asked for a protocol to predict aortic valve durability. What we brought is a
protocol, a pipeline that runs end to end, and an honest measurement of what the data we were
given can and cannot support."

---

## Slide 2 — The clinical problem (0:45)

**On the slide:** three numbers — 20.8% / 15.4% / 62.7%. Ten-year moderate-or-severe structural
valve deterioration, surgical and transcatheter, and all-cause mortality in the same cohort
(NOTION).

**Said:** "Bioprosthetic valves wear out, and not rarely: at ten years, one in five surgical
valves and one in seven transcatheter valves shows moderate or worse structural deterioration. But
look at the third number. Most of these patients die before ten years. Death competes with
deterioration, so any estimate that ignores that competition overstates how many valves fail. Every
incidence in our work is a cumulative incidence with death as a competing risk — never one minus
Kaplan–Meier."

---

## Slide 3 — Why surveillance is the real problem (0:45)

**On the slide:** a timeline of one valve: implant → reference echo → silence → detection. The
interval marked: median 1.0 years, maximum 4.3.

**Said:** "Deterioration is only ever *seen* at an echocardiogram. So the date in every registry is
the date somebody looked, not the date the valve started to fail — in our cohort, up to four years
apart. And surveillance is scheduled by the calendar: the ACC/AHA calendar and the ESC annual study
differ from each other by a factor of six in workload, and neither depends on the patient. The
consequence is the sentence this whole project turns on: **a patient with sparse follow-up is not a
low-risk patient, they are an unobserved one** — and a naive model will learn to leave them alone."

---

## Slide 4 — What the supplied data actually support (0:50)

**On the slide:** 117 patients → 52.1% reach an examination → 1.69 examinations each → **no
mortality data at all** → 32 assessable labels, 5 failures.

**Said:** "We measured the extract against the study schema instead of describing it. Half the
patients never reach an examination. There is no structured echo table anywhere — gradients, valve
area and dimensionless index exist only inside note text, which is exactly the abstraction problem
this room works on. There are no deaths recorded, so the competing risk is invisible. Thirty-two of
a hundred and seventeen patients can be labelled at all, five of them as failures. **We did not fit
a model to that, and we are reporting that as a finding rather than a shortfall.**"

---

## Slide 5 — The design (0:50)

**On the slide:** two layers. (A) Chart abstraction: note text → VARC-3 fields with an evidence
span, four statuses, clinician adjudication. (B) Risk model: landmark competing-risk model, death
competing, endpoint interval-censored.

**Said:** "The protocol has two layers, and they answer different questions. The first turns notes
into a VARC-3-adjudicated durability registry: every value carries the passage it came from, every
label is a proposal with one of four statuses, and two blinded cardiologists adjudicate with kappa
reported. The second is the risk model: at each echocardiogram, the probability that this valve
deteriorates within five years, with death as a competing event and the outcome interval-censored
between the last clean study and the one that found it."

---

## Slide 6 — How we tested it honestly (0:50)

**On the slide:** the degradation ladder — `ideal` → `no_age` → `year_resolution` → `single_echo`
→ `as_supplied` → **`as_received` (the real extract)**. Arrow marked −34% patients, −42% endpoint
rows.

**Said:** "With five events you cannot evaluate anything, so we built a synthetic cohort calibrated
to the published literature — and then ran the same unchanged pipeline down a ladder that strips
the data progressively until it looks like what we were given, with the real extract as the last
rung. That converts 'the data were poor' into a number: extract-quality surveillance loses 34% of
the patients who deteriorate and 42% of the endpoint rows. The patients are identical on every
rung. All that changes is whether anyone looked."

---

## Slide 7 — What it buys, and what it costs (0:50)

**On the slide:** AUC at 2 years 0.82 versus the calendar's 0.69; and the capacity table — the high
tier is 40% of patient-years and holds 66% of the deteriorations.

**Said:** "On the synthetic cohort the model reaches an AUC of 0.82 at two years against 0.69 for
the calendar schedule, and the decision curve shows a bounded window — between 5% and 46% predicted
risk — where acting on it beats both scanning everyone and changing nothing. In workload: the
tiered schedule is 463 fewer examinations per thousand patient-years than an annual study for
everyone, and the top tier, 40% of patient-years, contains two thirds of the deteriorations. And
the honest part: these models over-predict absolute risk by about half, so recalibration comes
before any clinical use. We say that in the document, not in the footnote."

---

## Slide 8 — What it would take to be real (0:55)

**On the slide:** 3,580 patients · external validation as its own study · recalibration ·
monitoring with an off switch · TRIPOD+AI, five gaps named.

**Said:** "To fit this model without shrinking it away takes 3,580 patients followed to five years
— we say so rather than rounding it away. External validation is scoped as a separate study,
because a laboratory that reports gradients two millimetres higher moves every prediction and no
internal split can detect that. Monitoring is in the protocol, with the triggers and an off switch
that returns the service to the guideline calendar. And we mapped the whole submission against
TRIPOD+AI and published the five items we fail. What changes on Monday morning is one thing: the
next echo date stops being a calendar entry and starts being a decision, taken with the risk and
the surveillance gap in view."

## Panel questions to drill (2 minutes, expect three)

| likely question | the short answer |
|---|---|
| "Your results are on simulated data — why should we believe them?" | We don't ask you to. The last rung of the ladder is the real extract, not a simulation of it; where the simulated bottom rung and the real one agree, the rungs in between can be believed. And the generator is validated by injecting known hazard ratios and recovering them with an independent fit: 70 of 75 intervals covered the injected value. |
| "Why not just follow the guideline?" | We keep it as the comparator, and below 5% predicted risk the decision curve says to do exactly that. The claim is narrower: between 5% and 46% a risk-guided schedule beats both scanning everyone and changing nothing. |
| "You have five events. What can you possibly conclude?" | Nothing about performance, and we conclude nothing about performance from them. What five events do support is a measurement of what the data lack, which is the finding we report. |
| "Why a mechanistic generator rather than CTGAN?" | A generative model learns the distribution you already hold. We needed structure the extract does not contain: you cannot learn a trajectory from a dataset with one trajectory per patient. |
| "The model over-predicts. Isn't that fatal?" | It is the top open item and it is a calibration problem, not a ranking problem — the ordering is sound, and recalibration updates the baseline risk without touching the coefficients. Until it is done, the thresholds stay provisional. |
| "Where does the LLM fit, and do notes leave the hospital?" | The LLM abstracts notes into VARC-3 fields with evidence spans, on-premises. Notes never leave the site; only aggregate monitoring statistics do. |
| "What about fairness?" | No fairness claim can be made from this extract — age is redacted, sex is inferable only from pronouns, ethnicity is absent, and we say so. The fairness risk specific to this endpoint is structural: a group imaged less often looks lower-risk, so the surveillance gap is displayed beside every prediction. |

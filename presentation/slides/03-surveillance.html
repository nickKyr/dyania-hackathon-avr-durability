[0:45] Target 105 words. This is the slide the whole project turns on. Point at the two cards on the right, then pause on the last line. "Here is how it works today. After surgery the first echo is at a year, after a transcatheter valve often at six months, then annually for both, and the interval is the same whatever the patient's risk. And here is where it breaks: those timely studies are simply not performed in about forty per cent of patients. The cost of finding it late is the difference between a planned operation and an emergency. Elective redo aortic valve replacement carries two and a half per cent operative mortality. The same operation, urgent or emergent, carries four point six to seventeen. And the cost of that delay is a fifty per cent higher long-term risk of death, and about thirty-six thousand dollars more per patient over three years." The full reference for the 40%, if anyone asks for it: Circulation 2026, doi:10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.126.081405. Be precise about the 40% if asked, and say it before you are asked if the panel looks sceptical. The published figure is for native aortic stenosis, where the guideline intervals are two years for moderate and one year for severe. It is the nearest measure we have of whether the same echo service delivers timely studies, and we quote it as that rather than as a prosthetic-valve figure. Do not claim that the patients who miss follow-up are the sicker ones. The published position runs both ways: symptomatic patients get more echo, while those who stop attending may be the well ones. The safe form of the claim is that a patient with sparse follow-up is not a low-risk patient but an unobserved one, and it is the condition stated on slide 4. Say plainly, if the cost figures are challenged, that they are United States estimates whose primary citations are still being attached, and that the clinical contrast above them, 2.5% against 4.6 to 17%, is the number to argue from. If asked about inter-observer variability, which the organisers list as a bottleneck: gradients vary between readers and between laboratories, which is why the protocol measures change from the patient's own reference study rather than an absolute level, and why external validation is scoped as a separate study.
